Paper explainer

AI Writes Faster Than Humans Can Review

Original paperHe, Agarwal, Denisov-Blanch, Azaletskiy, Koyejo, Vasilescu, “AI Writes Faster Than Humans Can Review: A Longitudinal Study of an Enterprise “2×” Mandate,” arXiv:2607.01904 version 3 (2026-11-10), not peer reviewed, CC BY 4.0 arXiv pageRead the HTML versionPDF

A company's CTO set a goal to double engineering productivity with AI within twelve months, measured as merged pull requests (PRs) per engineer per month. Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University, Stanford University, and elsewhere studied the company's records over more than two years. This page explains their paper.

English version of the Japanese page, written 2026-11-16 by microtaro and AI. Based on the full text and appendices of arXiv version 3 (2026-11-10); all numbers are from version 3. What the paper says is kept separate from microtaro's reading.

1Questions and answers (summary)

The paper asks three questions. For each one, here is the answer and the findings behind it. Reading this section alone gives you the main points. The small grey text says where each finding comes from.

Q1

How much and how fast did PR counts rise, and what produced the rise?

A

They rose through continued use of AI. The “2×” mandate alone did not raise them, and judged strictly the effect of AI is about 1.5×. The 2× target was reached ten months after the mandate.

Findings behind the answer

  • AI helps only a little at first. The gain grows the longer people keep using it. PRs per developer rose about 12% when a developer started using AI, and roughly doubled (1.99×) after nine months of use. Heavier users gained more. From comparing each developer before and after they started using AI
  • The “2×” mandate by itself did not raise PR counts. Throughput barely changed around the mandate date (about 10%). The mandate set off a wave of AI adoption, but the gain came only after each developer started using AI. Most of the gain came from continued use. From comparing the change at the mandate date with the change at each developer's own adoption month
  • Judged strictly, the effect of AI is about 1.5×, not 2×. PRs per developer did rise 2.09×, but that figure includes the company-wide trend over the same months. Net of that trend, the authors' main estimate is 1.46×. From subtracting the change seen in other developers in the same month
  • The work really increased; people did not just hit the number. PRs got larger (median 35 to 61 lines), and the added PRs were not skewed toward routine work. Extra working days explain at most a fifth of the gain. Whether the added code was worth it was not measured. From the authors' four checks

More in 2 (what changed with continued use) and 3 (PR counts)

Q2

Does the rise differ across people, code, and AI model generations?

A

Not clearly across engineering tiers. Managers and newer repositories gained much more. The effect of each AI model generation could not be separated.

Findings behind the answer

  • Managers started writing code again with AI (the authors' interpretation). The gain after adopting AI was largest for managers (+86%). The authors read this as AI letting managers who had shipped little code contribute more. Across the engineering tiers (+27% to +42%), the differences were not statistically significant. From before-and-after comparisons within each seniority tier, plus the authors' interpretation
  • Newer repositories gained a lot; older ones barely at all. Repositories created since 2022 gained +44%; older ones +12% (not significant). Newer repositories also use a more modern technology stack, so code age cannot be fully separated from technology. From before-and-after comparisons by when the repository was created
  • Which AI model raised PR counts could not be separated. Each model release coincided with a steep company-wide rise in AI use. Treating a month with no release as if it had one showed a similar effect. From splitting at release months, and a test with a fake release month

More in 4 (differences by people, code, and models)

Q3

How did the organization absorb the added PRs, and at what cost to code review and quality?

A

The cost fell on review. The company kept up by moving part of human review to AI, and more of the remaining human review became approval only. Short-term quality proxies did not get worse, but whether quality holds up in the long run is unknown.

Findings behind the answer

  • When authoring speeds up, review falls behind. Company-wide PR volume grew 3.1×, while the pool of reviewers grew only 1.5×, so the load per reviewer roughly doubled. From the company's month-by-month records
  • Review moved to AI, and more of the remaining human review became approval only. The share of PRs with an AI review rose from about 19% to about 84%, and the share with at least one human review fell from 89% to 68%. PRs with a human-written review comment fell from about 39% to about 21%, and most of the added review load went to approvals without comment. Human review did not disappear. From the company's month-by-month records. The paper reports these trends as they are; it does not model their cause
  • Quality did not visibly drop in the short run. Whether it holds up in the long run is unknown. Reverts and quick follow-up fixes did not rise. Defects, incidents, and maintainability were not measured. From short-horizon quality proxies
  • If you only count PRs, you will not notice that people are looking at less of the code. The success metric (merged PRs) recorded the speedup, but not the shift in who reviews the code. From the paper's summary

More in 5 (review), 6 (quality), and 8 (recommendations)

What the authors take from the three answers

The authors' conclusion (final section of the paper)

“organizational AI transformation unfolds through accumulated use and shifts in review practices rather than through the mandate alone. Human attention and judgment become the new bottleneck of the AI era.”

So the authors recommend

  • Treat a mandate as the start of an organizational transformation. The gain arrived over time, as each developer accumulated use. Plan over several quarters, and invest in intensity of use before demanding the target.
  • Pair the target metric with other signals. A count that becomes a target stops measuring what it was meant to measure (Goodhart's law).
  • Plan review capacity as carefully as authoring capacity. Set targets for review coverage and depth, decide in advance which PRs may be approved without a human review, and monitor which code humans no longer review.

The authors also say the result is evidence that “a near-doubling is attainable under favorable conditions and over a long enough horizon, rather than that it is typical, immediate, or free.” More in 8.

The story in one line

  1. The “2×” mandate
  2. Developers start using AI
  3. With continued use, PR counts rise
  4. Review falls behind
  5. Part of review moves to AI
  6. Short-term quality proxies stay flat

What the paper does not answer

The authors themselves say they could not measure the following.

  • Whether there are quality costs: only short-horizon proxies (merge rate, reverts, quick follow-up fixes) were measured. Defects, incidents, and maintainability were not.
  • Whether the added PRs were worth it: the value of the added code was not measured.
  • Whether it lasts: whether the gain levels off and whether the review shift persists needs longer panels and studies at other companies.

2What changed with continued use

The paper looks at change in two ways. Per developer, by months since they started using AI, PR counts grew the longer they used it. Company-wide, by calendar month, review moved toward AI as AI use spread.

The two views support different strengths of claim. The per-developer results are estimates of the effect of AI, made by comparing against other developers in the same month. The company-wide changes are the records laid out over time; the paper does not estimate whether continued AI use caused them.

Per developer: months since starting to use AI

  • PRs per developer rose about 12% when a developer started using AI, and kept rising with continued use.
  • Heavy users (the top quarter by usage) gained much more. The authors note that heavy use may result from high productivity as much as cause it.
  • The gain after adopting AI was largest for managers. The authors read this as AI letting managers who had shipped little code contribute more (4).

3Did PR counts really rise? (Q1)

PRs per developer rose 2.09×, but that figure includes the company-wide trend. The authors' main estimate of the effect of AI is 1.46×. Most of the gain came from continued use of AI; the mandate date itself contributed little.

The different “×” numbers in the paper

  • “2×” refers to the raw 2.09×. It is the simplest count and also includes the company-wide trend that affected developers who did not use AI.
  • The authors' main estimate subtracts all company-wide monthly changes: “We take this more conservative 1.46× as our main estimate.”
  • On the mandate's own metric — merged PRs, counting months with no PRs — the rise is 2.31×. The 246 developers active throughout also rose 2.3×, so the rise is not due to turnover.

Breaking down the gain: continued use is the largest part

Under the authors' preferred model, which subtracts all company-wide monthly changes, the total is +0.38 (1.46×). In both models, continued use supplies the larger share of the gain and the mandate only a small residual. The authors treat “the more you use it, the more you gain” as an association, not a cause, because productive developers may accumulate use faster.

Real work, or just hitting the target number?

A public target invites raising the number without doing more work. The authors ran four checks, and all of them support the “real work” reading.

  • PR types were classified from titles and similar fields; 33% of PRs could not be classified. AI tools write titles in a more uniform format, so ease of classification may also play a part.
  • The authors say the checks are “evidence against metric gaming,” but they “do not show the value of the added code.”

What the AI tools cost

Tool spend per PR rose more than tenfold. This is a lower bound: it leaves out Cursor spend and the time people spent writing and reviewing.

4Differences by people, code, and models (Q2)

Across the engineering tiers, differences in the gain were not significant; only managers gained much more. Newer repositories gained a lot, older ones barely at all. The effect of each AI model generation could not be separated.

  • Seniority: across the four engineering tiers from IC to Principal, differences in the gain were not statistically significant. Only Management stood out; the authors read this as AI letting managers who had shipped little code contribute more. The discussion also lists “managers returning to hands-on coding” as one of the new ways of working.
  • Code age: newer repositories gained a lot and older ones barely at all, and the difference itself is significant. Newer repositories also use a more modern technology stack, so code age cannot be fully separated from technology.
  • Model generations: the gain could not be attributed to particular model releases. Each release coincided with a steep company-wide rise in AI use. A fake release in August 2025, a month with no launch, showed as large an effect as the real ones — what looked like model effects cannot be told apart from the calendar trend.

5How AI review replaced part of human review (Q3)

PR volume grew and human review could not keep up. The company closed the gap partly with more human review, but mostly by moving review to AI. As a result, AI review replaced part of human review, and more of the remaining human review became approval without comment.

PR volume versus the number of reviewers

Who reviewed: people or AI

  • Within months of the mandate, the share of PRs with an AI review overtook the share with a human review.
  • In the paper's words: “Automated review thus replaced part of human review rather than only adding to it.” Even at the end, 68% of PRs still got a human review.
  • More of the remaining human review became approval without comment. The median reviewer's commented reviews stayed flat (about 3 per month) while their silent approvals roughly doubled, so most of the added load went to approvals without comment.
  • Late in the window, the company began routing PRs through AI review and auto-approval.

A missing comment does not prove the reviewer did not read the code. The paper only splits reviews by whether they include a human-written comment.

AI-authored PRs took longer in human review

The paper compared post-mandate PRs by the same author in the same month, controlling for size. Compared with other PRs, AI-authored PRs had:

MeasureAI-authored PRs
Human commentsabout 9% more
Time from first human review to mergeabout 20% longer
Time from first commit to mergeabout 22% longer
Review roundsno change
Chance of getting a human reviewno lower
  • Comments rose without more review rounds, so the authors read this as more reviewer engagement rather than more rework by the author.
  • They call it “the bottleneck one would expect when AI speeds up authoring while human review capacity stays fixed: work accumulates downstream.”
  • The company applies the AI label, which may reflect complexity that the controls only partly capture, so these comparisons are correlational.
  • Late in the window, PRs routed through AI review and auto-approval waited less for human review, which biases the measured difference toward zero; the authors call their estimate conservative.

How the company kept up with the added PRs

Review did not get faster. The company kept up by sending fewer PRs through human review with comments.

  • For all PRs, the same 90th-percentile time grew through 2025, peaked near 66 hours, and came down in early 2026.
  • It came down because the share of merged PRs that went through a human review with comments fell from a pre-mandate peak of about 48% to about 21%. PRs that still went through such review never got back to their earlier speed, as the chart shows.

“The company kept pace by routing work around human review rather than by reviewing faster.”

6Did quality hold up? (Q3)

The short-horizon proxies (merge rate, reverts, quick follow-up fixes) did not get worse. But the authors say that because defects, incidents, and maintainability were not measured, quality costs cannot be ruled out.

Merge rateFell only slightly, from 91% to 85%. AI-authored PRs merged at the same rate as other PRs, even though they were about 56% largerOnce review becomes lenient or automated, the gate itself has changed, so the merge rate says little about quality
RevertsThe share of PRs that are reverts declined slightly company-wide, and a developer's revert share did not rise with adoption. Revert PRs per developer rose 1.4×, more slowly than all PRs (1.8×)As PR volume grows, revert shares are diluted
Quick follow-up fixesWithin 7 days after an AI-authored PR merged, its author opened a bug-fix PR in the same repository 5.3 points more often than after other PRs — but also a feature PR 4.8 points more often. The fix-minus-feature difference was 0.3 points at 7 days and 0.6 points at 14 days, not significant. Authors were busier, not disproportionately busy fixingThe link between a PR and a later fix is coarse, so the authors report this only as suggestive
  • The company applies the AI label, and it may favor safer changes.
  • The authors summarize this as “evidence against a sudden drop in quality,” and also write: “we do not find any evidence of a sudden drop in quality, but we cannot rule out quality costs.”

Costs that may build up unseen

The authors write that this shift — less human review, partly replaced by AI review — describes a mechanism through which quality costs reported by other studies could accumulate unobserved. They list:

  • rising complexity and churn
  • diluted ownership of the code
  • cognitive and intent debt: losing understanding of the code and of why it was written that way

The reason: people examine less of the code, and the AI reviewers that replace them are only beginning to be evaluated.

7The company, and how the effect of AI was measured

The company

  • A mid-sized B2B software company (anonymized) that sells a software-as-a-service platform businesses use to support their customers. Its R&D organization has several hundred engineers.
  • The code combines a long-lived Ruby core, first written in 2012, with newer services in TypeScript, Python, and Go. Between 2024 and 2026 it grew from about 500 to over 1,000 repositories, and from about 4 to 6.5 million lines.
  • The company is AI-forward and provides commercial AI coding tools with no per-seat limits or token budgets. The authors call it an unusually favorable site and say the results are better read as an upper bound than as an industry average.
  • The company only shared data; it did not take part in the analysis or writing.

The data

  • January 2024 to April 2026 (28 months): 802 developers, 196,212 PRs (excluding bots), and 364 active repositories. Effect estimates use the 564 developers with at least three active months (451 who adopted AI and 113 who never did).
  • Usage logs from Cursor and Claude Code show who used AI, when, and how much. They do not record which model anyone used.
  • “AI-authored PRs” are counted by the company's own created-by-ai label, on 30.2% of all PRs. The researchers do not know how the label is applied, so comparisons using it are treated as correlational.

How the effect of AI was measured

This part is simplified. The paper uses several regression models and estimators.

  • Do not compare AI users with non-users directly. Early adopters already had much higher PR counts before adopting (nearly two standard deviations above the rest). A direct comparison would mistake that existing gap for the effect of AI.
  • Compare each developer with themselves before AI. Then subtract the change among developers whose AI use did not change in the same months, to remove company-wide shifts (the mandate, model releases, holidays, and so on). This is a difference-in-differences design; because developers adopted at different times, it is called a staggered difference-in-differences design.
  • Align on each developer's own adoption month, not on the mandate date. Aligning on the mandate would mix developers at different stages of adoption and blur the effect.
  • Check the assumption. The design assumes that, without AI, adopters would have moved like everyone else. Flat trends in the months before adoption support this, but cannot prove it.
  • Re-estimate with several methods. The size of the adoption effect ranged from +0.13 to +0.35 across estimators. It was smaller under the two estimators that compare adopters only with developers who had not yet adopted, and one of those was not significant. The authors explain that these estimators attribute the steep company-wide rise of early 2026 to the calendar rather than to AI, which understates an effect that grows with use. They conclude that the direction is clear but the size is uncertain.

An analogy: to judge a diet, do not compare the weight of people who dieted with people who did not. Take each dieter's change, and subtract the change in non-dieters over the same period (seasonal ups and downs, for example).

Reading the numbers: many of the paper's numbers are log-point differences. +0.54 means about 1.72×; +0.11 means about 1.12× (about 12% more).

8The authors' recommendations and the study's limits

The authors' recommendations

  • Treat a mandate as the start of an organizational transformation. The gain took time and came with new ways of working — managers returning to hands-on coding, and a review process rebuilt around AI review bots and auto-approval. The authors compare this with the IT productivity paradox of the 1970s to 1990s, when IT paid off only after a lag and alongside reorganized workflows and new skills. Plan over several quarters, and invest in intensity of use before demanding the target.
  • Pair the target metric with other signals. A count that becomes a target stops measuring what it was meant to measure (Goodhart's law).
  • Human attention and judgment become the bottleneck. At this company, review moved to AI, reviews with comments fell, and AI-authored PRs spent longer in human review — and the merged-PR metric showed none of it. So plan review capacity as carefully as authoring capacity: set targets for review coverage and depth alongside throughput, decide in advance which PRs may be approved without a human review, and monitor which code humans no longer review.
  • Open questions: how to allocate scarce human attention across AI output, and how much a human must understand before code ships.

“Human attention and judgment become the new bottleneck of the AI era.”

9How the shape of trust changed (microtaro's reading)

This section is microtaro's reading, not the paper's claim. The paper talks about review and about human attention and judgment; it does not frame its findings in terms of trust.

  • This is real company data that bears directly on this theme's question: can the trust that came from people reading code move to another basis? As continued AI use spread, part of the job of checking code moved from people reading it to AI review and silent human approval. Short-horizon quality proxies did not get worse. The long run is unknown.
  • The move was triggered by review falling behind the volume of PRs. In the authors' words, the company routed work “around human review.” Their advice to decide in advance which PRs may be approved without a human review, and to monitor code that humans no longer review, reads to microtaro as: decide where trust is secured before the fact, not after.
  • The success metric did not show the change in trust. “More PRs” and “merged” say nothing about who checked the code. It is the same as saying that “it runs” is not, by itself, a reason to trust code.
  • The paper feels unresolved because it shows a risky mechanism but not harm. The abstract ends on “strong evidence” that AI raises PR throughput, while the title and conclusion stress that human review cannot keep up. Neither quality costs nor the value of the added code was measured. Pushed to its core, the paper's answer is: it is not known yet, so measure where humans are no longer looking.
  • A caution for people building alone with AI. This is about an R&D organization of several hundred engineers, which is a different situation. What carries over directly is: when you can build faster, checking falls behind; and what is not checked does not show up in the numbers.
