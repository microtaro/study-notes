Human-AI Synergy in Agentic Code Review

Original paperZhong, Noei, Zou, Adams, “Human-AI Synergy in Agentic Code Review,” arXiv:2603.15911 version 3 (2026-10-30), not peer reviewed, arXiv non-exclusive distribution license arXiv pageRead the HTML versionPDF

Researchers collected 278,790 code review conversations from 300 open-source projects and compared what human reviews and AI reviews actually contain. AI agents handled a large volume of defect detection, while humans carried “why did you do it this way?” questions and the passing on of project knowledge. The data shows how their strengths differ, and gives clues on how to combine them. The authors are from Queen's University in Canada. This page explains their paper.

1Questions and answers (summary)

The paper asks three questions. For each one, here is the answer and the findings behind it. Reading this section alone gives you the main points. The small grey text says where each finding comes from.

Q1

What are the similarities and differences between AI and human review comments?

A

For both, the most common type is code improvement. On top of that, AI agents point out defects far more often (30–40% of their comments, versus 7–8% for humans) and put a weakness and its fix in one comment. Humans write a wider range, including “why” questions and passing on project knowledge. Human comments are easier to read.

Findings behind the answer

  • Over 95% of AI comments are code improvement or defect detection. Defect detection made up 30–40% of AI comments, versus 7–8% of human comments. From the main text and the numbers printed inside Figure 5
  • Humans ask “why” and pass on knowledge. Understanding questions were 31% of comments when humans reviewed human-written code and 17% for AI-written code. Knowledge transfer, such as pointing to existing code or conventions, was 4–6%. From the main text
  • AI agents put the weakness and the fix in one comment. Comments that name a weakness and suggest a fix without asking a question were 87.7–94.5% for AI agents and 31.3–31.9% for humans. Reading-ease scores were 67.2–72.8 for humans and 43.0–43.9 for AI agents. From Table IV and the main text

Q2

How do conversations differ between human and AI reviews, and was the code changed afterward?

A

85–87% of AI-initiated reviews end after the first comment. When humans review AI-written code, conversations run 11.8% longer. The share of conversations followed by a related code change after the first comment was highest when humans reviewed human-written code (47.6%).

Findings behind the answer

  • AI comments usually stand alone. 85.2–86.7% of AI-initiated reviews ended at the AI's first comment with no follow-up. When humans reviewed human-written code, 48.9% had multiple rounds. From the analysis of interaction sequences
  • Humans reviewing AI-written code talk longer. 11.8% longer on average than when reviewing human-written code. The paper also gives an example where an AI agent offered to do something it could not do (open a separate PR), dragging the conversation out. From the main text, Figure 7, and the example in Figure 8
  • Code was changed most often when humans reviewed human-written code. After the first comment, with no further exchange, a related code change followed in 47.6% of human-on-human conversations, 30.9% of AI-on-AI, 24.5% of AI-on-human, and 17.5% of human-on-AI. From the main text and Figure 7

Q3

How often are human and AI code suggestions adopted, and how does code change once they are?

A

AI agents make far more suggestions (88,011 versus 25,673), but only 16.6% were adopted, versus 56.5% for humans. The most common reasons for non-adoption were an incorrect suggestion (28.7%) and the developer choosing a different fix (24.0%). Adopted AI suggestions increased code complexity and size more than human ones.

Findings behind the answer

  • AI agents suggest a lot. AI agents made suggestions in 56.6% of their conversations (88,011 suggestions); humans in 20.8% (25,673). From the main text
  • Adopted: 56.5% of human suggestions, 16.6% of AI suggestions. The gap was largest for suggestions that pass on project-specific knowledge, such as renamed classes and repository conventions, which AI agents rarely give. From Table V and the main text
  • Adopted AI suggestions tend to add complexity and lines. Human suggestions slightly lowered complexity metrics; AI suggestions slightly raised them. Some tools went the other way (Greptile lowered complexity). From Table VII and the main text

What the authors take from the three answers

The authors' conclusion (abstract, introduction, and conclusion)

AI agents can screen for defects at scale. Human oversight remains essential for checking suggestion quality and for the contextual feedback AI agents lack. Assign review roles to humans and AI agents according to their strengths, and combine them.

“while AI agents can scale defect screening, human oversight remains critical for ensuring suggestion quality and providing contextual feedback that AI agents lack”

To that end, the authors recommend (mainly to tool builders and researchers)

  • Assign review roles by strength. The results give practitioners a basis for giving defect screening to AI agents and intent and knowledge to humans.
  • Help AI agents check developer intent. Mechanisms for asking clarifying questions and retrieving project-specific context can help AI agents address intent-related feedback.
  • Give AI suggestions project-specific information. Study whether build configurations, prior review decisions, and similar information change suggestion accuracy and adoption.
  • Evaluate AI comments for readability too. Not only for technical coverage, but for readability and clarity.
  • Keep context across multi-turn conversations. Stronger context tracking and self-verification can help AI agents act on reviewers' concerns.

For practitioners, the authors add that adopted AI suggestions should be reviewed carefully so they do not degrade maintainability. More in 8.

The story in one line

  1. AI agents join code review
  2. AI agents handle much of the defect detection
  3. Humans carry intent and knowledge
  4. 16.6% of AI suggestions are adopted
  5. Humans check before adopting
  6. Split by strength and combine

What the paper does not answer

The authors themselves say the following are not measured or cannot be separated.

  • Whether bugs went down: code quality was measured with file-level metrics of size, complexity, coupling, and cohesion. Architecture-level changes, security vulnerabilities, and fault proneness were not measured.
  • AI inside human accounts: humans and AI agents were told apart by account. Content from human accounts may include AI-generated work, which limits how precisely the human-AI difference is known.
  • Tool versions: GitHub comments do not reveal which version of a tool was used. The data runs to November 2025.

2What changes when humans review AI-written code

When humans reviewed AI-written code rather than human-written code, they asked fewer “why” questions and more often gave a fix without explaining. Conversations ran longer, yet the share followed by a code change after the first comment was the lowest of all.

Numbers from the main text, Table IV, and Figure 7.

  • When humans reviewed AI-written code, two thirds of conversations went past the first human comment, into human-AI back-and-forth.
  • At later stages, code changes became less frequent and continued discussion more frequent (66.4% to 77.5%).
  • The authors suggest the extra rounds may include checking whether the AI agent can actually do what it offered.

3How human and AI comments differ (Q1)

For both humans and AI agents, code improvement is the most common type. AI agents point out defects far more often; humans also ask “why” and pass on knowledge. AI agents bundle a weakness with its fix; humans ask questions more often, and their comments are easier to read.

  • Humans also mostly suggest improvements: 49–55% of human comments were code improvement, the most common type, as for AI agents.
  • Humans pass on knowledge: 4–6% of human comments pointed to existing code or settings. The authors sum up that humans clarify design intent through questions and share project-specific references, while AI agents rarely give references to support their fixes.
  • Finer categories: four statistically significant differences appeared within code improvement and defect detection, all with small effect sizes. For example, AI agents reviewing AI-written code gave more documentation feedback (29%, versus 18% for humans reviewing human-written code).
  • Readability: reading-ease scores (0–100, higher is easier) were 67.2–72.8 for human comments and 43.0–43.9 for AI comments, a large difference.
  • Questions: humans often check by asking; when humans reviewed human-written code, 21.1% of comments were questions only.

4How conversations unfold, and whether code changed (Q2)

Most AI-initiated reviews ended after the first comment. When humans reviewed AI-written code, conversations ran longer. The share followed by a code change after the first comment was highest when humans reviewed human-written code, and lower whenever AI agents were involved.

  • Human on human: 99.8% of conversations ended without any AI participation, averaging 1.76 rounds; 48.9% had multiple rounds.
  • AI-initiated: 85.2–86.7% ended at the AI's first comment with no follow-up, 30.6% fewer rounds than human-on-human conversations.
  • When the AI keeps responding: in AI-on-human conversations, the share ending in a code change rose at each successive AI turn (24.5%, 32.8%, 35.3%). But after a human replied to the AI and another AI-human exchange followed, it fell from 19.1% to 7.2%.

This part is simplified. The paper extracts the sequence of who commented and models it as a finite state machine. “Changed” means a change addressing the comment that was kept in the main branch when the PR merged.

6Differences between AI tools

Most tools behaved like AI agents overall. Some stood out, such as a tool that almost only points out defects (Cursor) and one whose adopted suggestions lowered complexity (Greptile). Treating AI review as one thing hides these differences.

7The data and how it was classified

Data

  • Public GitHub projects with at least 100 stars, at least one closed PR per month from 2022 to November 2025, and at least 100 PRs reviewed by AI agents: 300 projects and 54,330 closed PRs.
  • 278,790 inline code review conversations, each attached to specific changed lines.
  • GitHub marks accounts as users or bots but not whether a bot is AI. The authors checked each bot's official documentation and identified 16 AI review bots, counting their reviews only from the date each announced agentic capabilities (GitHub Copilot from 2025-05-19).

Classification and judgment

This part is simplified. The paper specifies statistical tests and decision procedures in detail.

  • Feedback type: the first comment of each conversation was classified with an LLM (GPT-4.1-mini) into Bacchelli and Bird's nine 2013 categories, dropping low-confidence labels (96.4% of conversations had high confidence). On 383 manually labeled comments, agreement was almost perfect (κ=0.85).
  • Comment form: whether a comment is kind, asks a question, names a weakness, or suggests a fix, labeled with an LLM (κ=0.9).
  • Code changed: for suggestions, whether the committed code was closer to the suggestion than to the original; for natural-language comments, an LLM judgment (all 50 manually checked cases agreed).
  • Changes after adoption: 111 metrics measured with a static analysis tool (SciTools Understand) before and after each adopted suggestion.

8The authors' recommendations and the study's limits

The authors' recommendations

The authors present their results as guidance for assigning review tasks by the strengths of humans and AI agents, for streamlining review to get the most from human-AI collaboration, and for understanding where AI code modifications work best. Mainly to tool builders and researchers, they recommend:

  • Bridge the gap between AI agents and human reviewers: human feedback focuses on clarifying developer intent. Research methods that let AI agents infer and articulate intent, through clarification and project-specific context retrieval.
  • Give suggestions project-specific context: study whether build configurations, prior review decisions, and similar information change suggestion accuracy and adoption.
  • Evaluate AI feedback for readability and clarity: not only for technical coverage.
  • Keep context across multiple exchanges: conversations where humans review AI-written code are the longest, yet least often lead to code changes. Strengthen context tracking and self-verification.

As future work, they aim to improve AI agents' context awareness, explore multi-agent mechanisms for sharing project knowledge, and reduce incorrect suggestions.

The study's limits (as the authors state them)

LimitDetails
Humans and AI told apart by accountContent from human accounts may include AI-generated or AI-modified work, which stays in the human category and may affect the estimated differences
File-level quality metricsSize, complexity, coupling, and cohesion; architecture-level changes, security vulnerabilities, and fault proneness are not captured
Other factorsRepository, language, PR size, code churn, developer experience, and the AI tool used may influence the metric changes
Tool and version differencesTools differ, and GitHub comments do not reveal agent versions, so other tools or versions may give different results

9What part of human review can move to other forms? (microtaro's reading)

This section is microtaro's reading, not the paper's claim. The paper does not frame its findings in terms of trust.

  • Human review carried two jobs. This theme asks whether the trust that came from people reading code can move to another basis. One view is that looking at code is itself the basis of trust, and it can move to other forms such as tests. In this paper's data, human review did at least two jobs: screening for defects, and asking “why” and passing on project knowledge.
  • Defect screening can move to AI review and tests. 30–40% of AI comments point out defects, far more than humans' 7–8%. In (When) Should a Software Factory be Lights-off?, AI agents beat humans on severe bugs, and their bug-finding could be measured with a benchmark of past bugs. This is the part that can move from looking to tests and AI review.
  • “Why” questions and knowledge transfer show up in neither tests nor AI review. 17–31% of human comments were understanding questions, and knowledge-transfer suggestions were the most often adopted (75.8%). AI agents hardly produce these. They do not show up in a test result or an AI review comment. Moving them would take another form, such as writing down design intent and conventions — that is microtaro's view.
  • Humans stop asking “why” when reviewing AI-written code. Understanding questions fell from 31% to 17%, and fix-only comments rose from 11.7% to 32.6%. The drop in human reviews with comments in AI Writes Faster Than Humans Can Review may have been this checking part.
  • Check AI suggestions before adopting them. 28.7% of unadopted AI suggestions were incorrect, and adopted ones tended to add complexity. When building alone with AI too, the use this paper supports is to check whether an AI reviewer's suggestion fits your intent before taking it.
