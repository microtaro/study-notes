How the Claude Code team does code review

Original articleAddy Osmani, Morgan Westlee Lunt, Qing Wang, “How the Claude Code team does code review,” claude.dev Blog (Engineering), 2026-10-15. An article in which Anthropic's Claude Code team describes its own practice. It is not a peer-reviewed paper. The article page shows no license. Original article (claude.dev)

About 90% of Claude Code's code is written by Claude. That is more than people can review by reading every line. The article describes how the Claude Code team kept review but moved most of the reading to machines, and limited human approval to the changes that carry risk.

1Questions and answers (summary)

This is not a research paper. It is one team's account of how it works. The questions the article answers are grouped here into five. The small grey text says which section of the article each point comes from (section names as in the original).

Q1

What does the Claude Code team aim for in code review, and how does it measure it?

A

To cut the time people spend on review without more reverts or incidents. The target is for nearly every PR (a request to merge a change) to be merged with at most one person involved, and that person may be the author.

What in the article the answer rests on

  • Human time comes in two kinds.Time spent reviewing, and time spent fixing what bad changes broke. Cutting the first while the second grows only moves human time elsewhere. So the revert rate and the incident rate serve as guardrails.From the article's “The scarce resource: human time and attention”
  • The question changed.Not “did a person read the code?” but “what could this change break, and which check, automated or human, is best placed to catch it?”From the article's “Rethinking the purpose of code review”

Q2

What checks does a change pass through before it is merged and reaches users?

A

Not a single gate but stacked layers of checks. Before the PR is opened, Claude checks its own work. Every PR then goes through required CI, AI review, security review, and human approval scaled to risk. After merge, flags and a gradual release keep each change easy to roll back.

What in the article the answer rests on

  • Other agents vote to filter the AI review's findings.Several agents look for bugs in parallel, and a separate set of agents votes on whether each finding is real. Only findings with enough votes appear on the PR.From item 2 of the article's “Layer by layer”
  • An unresolved finding blocks the merge.Each finding from the bug review or the security review has to be either fixed or closed with a reason.From items 2 and 3 of the article's “Layer by layer”

Q3

When is human approval needed, and from how many people?

A

It depends on the change's risk. Low risk needs nobody, provided the automated checks pass; an ordinary change needs one person; the most dangerous areas need two. The tier is set by what the change contains, not by who wrote it, and an AI approval does not count as a human one.

What in the article the answer rests on

  • A classifier reads the change and sets the tier.A second classifier decides whether a change needs a person regardless of its tier. Paths that owners mark as sensitive, and CI workflow files, always need a person.From item 5 of the article's “Layer by layer”
  • An approval applies to one specific commit.If a commit the system cannot vouch for arrives later, such as an agent's push or a merge resolved by hand, the change must be approved again.From item 5 of the article's “Layer by layer”

Q4

When AI writes the code and AI reviews it, the back-and-forth can go on without end. What did the team do about that?

A

It added a cap: after three rounds, the reviewer asks the author to rethink the approach. After the first few rounds, only the new diff is reviewed again. On internal PRs, the share that passed after a single round of review and fixes rose from 65% to 78%.

What in the article the answer rests on

  • One PR went past 50 rounds.A CLI PR went back and forth between review and fixes more than 50 times in one night, until its author stopped it. About 59% of the code flagged again and again had already been there at the first review.From the article's “How we make the reviewer better”
  • Minor findings cost more when the author is an agent.A person can wave off a minor comment in seconds, but an agent fixes it. That adds a commit, and the review needs another round.From the article's “How we make the reviewer better”

Q5

Once machines do most of the reading, what is left for people?

A

Deciding whether to build something at all, reviewing risky code and designs that others will copy, reading the evidence from the checks, and owning every change that ships.

What the authors want to say

The authors' conclusion (the article's opening and its last section, “Where this is going”)

Review was not dropped. Most of the reading moved to machines, human time and attention went to where the risk is, and mistakes became quick to find and undo. The goal stays the same: less human time per change, and no more reverts than now.

So, for teams that want to adopt this, the authors recommend the following

  • Before the PR: give the agent checks it can run itself.Write how to check the work in a short CLAUDE.md, and run formatting and lint through hooks. Run a review locally as well. Move review comments you keep writing into REVIEW.md.
  • On every PR: stack checks that can block the merge.Make CI required, add AI review and automated security review, and resolve every finding before merging. Match the review effort to the risk, and cap the number of rounds. Start the AI reviewer on one repository and tune REVIEW.md for a few weeks before expanding.
  • Approval: write down the risk tiers.Set the tier by the change, start strict, and relax as evidence comes in. Make sure authentication, billing, CI and release workflows, and installers always need a person. Do not let bot approvals stand in for human ones, and withdraw approvals when new commits arrive.
  • After merge: keep changes easy to undo, and measure.Use a merge queue, flags, internal use first, a gradual release, and rollback in one step. Turn incidents and reverts into a set of test cases, and re-score the reviewer whenever it changes. Measure human time, with the revert and incident rates as guardrails.

The list in the article's “How you can adopt this process,” regrouped into four points. More in 9.

How the argument runs

  1. Changes surge; people cannot read every line
  2. Ask what the change could break
  3. Stack the checks; machines do the reading
  4. Scale human approval to risk
  5. Find mistakes fast and undo them

What the article does not answer

What the authors themselves say is not built or not settled yet:

  • Automatic tightening: there is no mechanism yet (a circuit breaker) that tightens approval automatically when the revert or incident numbers move.
  • Noticing bad merges: this still depends on the author, the on-call engineer, and dashboards.
  • How far merging without people can go: code owners decide which low-risk changes may merge on automated checks alone, and that line is still moving.

3What the team aims for, and how it measures it (Q1)

The aim is for nearly every PR to be merged with at most one person involved. Whether human time was really saved is checked by watching that the revert and incident rates do not rise.

  • What set it off was volume: in Anthropic's monorepo (one repository holding much of the company's code), changes per day went from about 2,500 in May to more than 30,000 in September. The Claude Code repository is separate, but it grew in the same way.
  • Scope: the Claude Code repository, which holds the CLI, the desktop and web apps, and the mobile apps.
  • What is measured: human approvals per PR (the median and the 95th percentile), human comments, and how long PRs wait for people.
  • Guardrails: the revert rate (a revert undoes a change that was merged) and the incident rate. If review time falls but time spent fixing breakage rises, human time has only moved.

4The layers of checks a change passes through (Q2)

Every layer catches some problems and lets others through. Stacking layers leaves fewer problems that get through all of them, and whatever still gets through is found and rolled back quickly. People are involved only in the approval layer, which is scaled to risk.

Inside the AI review

  • What it looks at: the diff, read against the whole repository and the intent stated in the PR.
  • How findings are filtered: several agents look for bugs in parallel, and a separate set of agents votes on each finding. Only findings with enough votes are posted. Findings about style, diagnostics, or cleanup alone are dropped. The team adopted this filter after A/B testing it on its own PRs.
  • Labels: each finding carries one of Important, Nit, or Pre-existing. Pre-existing marks a bug the PR did not introduce, so that it does not look like the author's fault.
  • Responding to findings: fix it, or close it with a reason. While it stays open, the change cannot merge.
  • Depth: administrators choose Quick, Standard, or Deep for each repository.
  • Existing code too: the review agents also go through existing code every night.

When the AI review's research preview started, the share of internal PRs with substantive findings rose from 16% to 54%. PRs over 1,000 lines usually get findings; small PRs usually get none. The public product is Code Review for Claude Code (on the Team and Enterprise plans), and /code-review runs a lighter version of it locally.

5Human approval is set by the change's risk (Q3)

A documentation fix and a change to the auto-update mechanism carry very different risks. So the number of human approvals changes with the change's risk tier. What sets the tier is the content of the change, not the person who wrote it.

TierWhat kind of changeHuman approval
LowDocumentation, wording, internal-only features, changes that only add something behind a flagNot needed (if the automated review and security checks pass)
MediumOrdinary product changes, including changes to sensitive areas such as caching, auto-update, and system prompts1 person
HighThe most dangerous code: a small set of paths that always need two people2 people

The table in item 5 of the article's “Layer by layer,” restated in microtaro and AI's own words.

  • A classifier sets the tier: one classifier reads the change and picks a tier, and another decides whether the change needs a person regardless of tier. Paths that owners mark as sensitive, and CI workflow files, always need a person.
  • The light path is for trusted authors only: anyone outside the repository's trusted authors (mainly the team that owns the code) needs a second approval.
  • AI approvals do not count: an agent's approval does not count as a human approval. Automated review can pass a low-risk change, but it cannot fill a slot that requires a person.
  • The person who directed the work is accountable: on a PR written by an agent, the person who directed the agent counts as one of the required approvers.
  • Approval is per commit: an approval applies to one specific commit. If a new commit arrives that the system cannot vouch for (an agent's push, a merge resolved by hand), the change must be approved again.

6Improving the AI reviewer and ending the back-and-forth (Q4)

The reviewer is tested every day on a set of cases built from past incidents, and the team also measures how many unhelpful findings it produces. For back-and-forth between AIs that never ends, the team added a cap on rounds and other changes, and more PRs now pass after a single round of fixes.

How the reviewer's quality is measured

  • Incidents become test cases: the team gathered PRs that had caused incidents or reverts, together with the fixes that followed, and engineers who know the code confirmed each bug and where it was. The reviewer is tested on this set (an eval) every day.
  • Unhelpful findings are measured too: catching many bugs is easy if you may also report lots of things that are not bugs. So the team sorts findings by how people responded: fixed it, said it was wrong, said it was deliberate, or found it did not matter. It also runs the reviewer on PRs known to be clean and counts reports that could lead to harmful fixes.
  • Order of priority: first, catching real bugs; second, reducing the number of review rounds.

When review rounds do not end

  • What happened: a CLI PR went back and forth between review and fixes more than 50 times in one night, until its author stopped it.
  • What the team found: running git blame (a command that shows when and by whom each line was written) on the repeatedly flagged code showed that about 59% of it had been there since the first review. The reviewer was not finding new bugs from that PR; it was finding old bugs, a few more each time.
  • What changed: after three rounds, the reviewer asks the author to step back and rethink the approach. After the first few rounds, only the new diff is reviewed again. The reviewer names the kind of bug rather than one spot, so that every instance gets fixed. Findings already raised are not repeated.
  • What is tracked: the number of rounds until a PR passes, alongside recall (the share of real bugs the reviewer catches).

Instructions for the reviewer: CLAUDE.md and REVIEW.md

  • CLAUDE.md: describes what the project is (its technology, conventions, goals, and what it does not do). It is loaded on every review, so it is kept short and points to other documents for detail. The reviewer also flags code that breaks its rules, and changes that would make it out of date.
  • REVIEW.md: written by code owners to tell the reviewer what matters in their area. The article says a good REVIEW.md is short and defines what “failure” means in that repository. Its examples: for documentation, failure means stating something untrue; for configuration, sending things to the wrong place. It caps the number of minor comments (nits), lists paths that need no review, asks for file-and-line evidence instead of guesses from names, and has the summary start with counts and state plainly when there is nothing to fix.
  • Fix repeats at the source: if the same finding keeps coming up, change a skill, a lint rule, or an instruction file. When a review misses something, ask what the model did not have in front of it, and put the fix into CLAUDE.md, a skill, or a new check.

7The work that stays with people when machines do the reading (Q5)

What stays with people is judgment and accountability. They decide whether to build something, look at the risky areas and at designs others will copy, read the evidence from the checks, and own the changes that ship.

  • Deciding whether to build it: direction, taste, and scope.
  • Reviewing risky code: billing, auto-update, CI workflows, and the like.
  • Reviewing designs others will copy: designs that other people will take as a template.
  • Reading the evidence: test output, the commands run and their results, screenshots, and the reviewer's findings. If you cannot say what the checks might miss, do not approve quickly; ask for a proper review.
  • Owning the outcome: whoever typed it, every change that ships has someone accountable for it.

The person who merged it fixes it

When a small or medium change causes a problem, the team's rule is that the person who merged it usually fixes it (in the article's words, “You land it, you own it”). This is not about blame: the person fixing it knows the most about it. The article gives four reasons the rule works.

  • Changes are small, so they are easy to review, to trace, and to revert.
  • Flags, reverts, and gradual releases make most mistakes undoable.
  • The bar depends on how far the damage could spread: code with a wider impact gets more people and more checks.
  • Regressions (things that used to work and stopped) show up quickly through telemetry (data sent back about usage and faults) and internal use.

How understanding of the code is shared

  • Besides finding problems, review used to have a second job: spreading understanding of the code across the team. Once machines do most of the reading, the question of how to keep that job done remains.
  • There is no formal mechanism. No one on the team holds all of Claude Code in their head, and no one thinks anyone needs to.
  • To understand a part, people have Claude write an explanation of it, and some have it build a small page with diagrams. Because the team works in Slack channels anyone can see, people can watch what others are building while it is being built.

When the direction changes

Agents make starting work cheap, so some work gets started that should not have been. Usually someone in the channel asks why, points to an earlier decision, or suggests a simpler route, and the author closes or reworks the PR. Because the code was quick to write, the article says, closing the PR does not feel like a loss. Its advice is to raise a different direction early in the thread, not as a blocking comment on a finished PR.
